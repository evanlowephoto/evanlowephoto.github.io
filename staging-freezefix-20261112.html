The real detector · 100% on-device

Deepfake Detection and Digital Forensics, at full power.

Bring in any video — new or old — and this detector goes to work on it entirely on your device, layer after layer, with nothing uploaded and nothing saved. It reads micro-expressions and tracks gaze, counts blinks, checks lip-sync against the voice, hunts edits and stutters frame by frame, and follows breathing, head movement, and posture. It decodes Morse code hidden in the blinks and Morse tones hidden in the soundtrack, writes both messages out in plain text, and compares them side by side. And it measures the voice itself — cadence, pitch, micro-tremor, and spectral consistency — the patterns synthetic and spliced audio struggles to hold together. A further layer — the Voice Lie Detector box — combines micro-tremors, pitch/frequency shifts, intensity changes and spikes, speech-rate changes, and pausing into a single stress-pattern score for each stretch of speech, and flags the moment those cues fire together in real time. It is a voice lie detector, but not fully 100% accurate: those cues are commonly associated with lying and are the same cues used in voice lie detectors, but they measure stress and arousal only, do not prove someone is lying, and stress has honest causes too. Every layer is then scored in a final verdict summary against sourced human averages, so you can see plainly what behaves like a real person in a real recording, and what does not. Voice Lie Detector

A voice lie detector — but not fully 100% accurate.

These stress cues — micro-tremors, pitch/frequency shifts, intensity changes and spikes, speech-rate changes, and pausing — are commonly associated with lying and are the same cues used in voice lie detectors. But they do not prove someone is lying: stress is not the same as lying, and stress has honest causes too — nervousness, tiredness, illness, emotion, being put on the spot, or simply being recorded. A person can sound stressed and be telling the truth, and a person can lie calmly. No voice cue, alone or combined into a score, proves someone is lying.

Everything in this one box is the stress-signals layer. The cues are combined per speech segment, because they mean more together than alone: a single shaky moment is ordinary, but a segment where the voice trembles more, the pitch steps, the volume spikes, and the speech rate shifts and the pausing turns unusual at the same time is a stronger stress pattern. Each segment gets one combined score out of 100 — four contributions of up to 25 points each, shown separately so you can see exactly what drove the score. How the score is built (per segment — a run of speech bursts separated by pauses under 1.2 s, at least about a second long). Micro-tremor (0–25): pitch jitter inside the segment — how much F0 wobbles cycle to cycle; more wobble scores higher. Pitch/frequency shifts (0–25): F0 variability plus abrupt step changes (>25% between nearby voiced frames). Intensity (0–25): how much the volume (RMS) varies and swings inside the segment, including intensity spikes — the volume suddenly jumping to more than about 2.2× the level of the moment just before it. Speech rate & pausing (0–25): estimated syllables per second (counted from volume-envelope peaks) moving away from a calm ~4 syllables/s, speech-rate shifts (the rate changing sharply between the first and second half of the segment, or away from the clip's typical rate), plus the pause pattern: long pauses (0.9 s or more inside a segment), sudden pauses mid-sentence (a pause of 0.45 s or more that cuts in while the segment is still going, rather than at its end), and pause clustering (pauses bunching together — 3 or more within about 5 seconds). 55 or more = elevated stress pattern for that segment. The score is a pattern summary, not a probability of anything.

Morse code in the blinks

Every both-eyes closure from the eye readout is re-read as a Morse element — short blink, dot; long blink, dash — grouped into letters, words, and punctuation by the pauses between blinks, and decoded with the full International Morse code set (the ITU standard): all 26 letters, digits 0–9, punctuation (. , ? ' ! / ( ) & : ; = − _ " $ @), and prosigns. Prosigns are sent run together, with no letter gap, so they decode distinctly in brackets — [R] received / roger, [AR] end of message, [CQ] calling any station, [SOS] distress — never as the separate letters they are made of.

Coded or natural?

No assessment yet. Natural blinks land seconds apart and decode as isolated single letters (mostly E for a dot, T for a dash). Morse code in the sound — audio tones & beeps

The soundtrack is listened to on this device only — never recorded, uploaded, or saved. A steady, narrow tone (a beep, typically a few hundred Hz) is timed while the video plays: how long the tone stays on becomes a dot or a dash, and the silences between tones group them into characters and words, decoded with the same full International Morse set as the blinks — letters A–Z, digits 0–9, punctuation, and prosigns decoded distinctly in brackets: [CQ], [AR], [R], [SOS]. Keep the clip unmuted with the volume up: the tones are measured from playback, exactly like the lip-sync layer. Speech cadence

Speech is split into bursts (sound above the speech level) and the pauses between them. Natural cadence is uneven — bursts and pauses vary in length as people think, breathe, and emphasize. A run of 6 or more bursts whose lengths and gaps are both metronome-even (variation under about 13%) is flagged as a synthetic-cadence pattern: generated and stitched speech often keeps a steadier beat than a person does.

Micro-tremor — the tiny shakes in a real voice

A living voice is never perfectly steady: the pitch wobbles slightly from cycle to cycle (jitter, typically a few tenths of a percent up to about 1% in a healthy voice) and the loudness carries a small natural tremor. Each 1.5-second stretch of voiced speech is measured for both. A stretch with almost no wobble at all — jitter under 0.18% and a nearly flat loudness envelope — is flagged as micro-tremor unnaturally absent: synthesized voices are often too clean in exactly this way.

Spectral consistency

The spectrum — where the sound's energy sits across frequencies — is summarized by its centroid and flatness. Within one sentence the recording chain doesn't change, so the spectrum shouldn't either. A centroid that jumps by more than about 45% and stays there for 300 ms inside a sentence is logged as a spectral shift: the audio quality changed mid-sentence, as happens when audio is spliced, re-generated, or swapped between sources.

Final verdict summary

Every layer from the analysis above, side by side: what was measured in this clip, the normal average range from a reputable source, and whether that layer landed inside or outside the average. A few outside layers are normal — genuine footage trips one or two all the time. This summary only flags a video as showing too many anomalies when several scorable layers are outside the average at once. 1% false-positive ceiling (Evan's bar): the flag bar is set so clean footage is flagged less than 1 in 100 — a ceiling, not a quota, and never an automatic one-in-100 flag. One known-clean clip cannot prove a 1% rate; the bar is calibrated so that clip reads inside, and a real clean set is needed before any 1% claim is made. Breathing, prolonged eye closures, and gaze breaks are shown as counts only — their measured values stay in the table below, in the stats, and in the batch counts, but they are not counted toward the flag. The verdict is carried instead by blink duration, alongside blink rate, time between blinks, and lip-sync. Edits / cuts no longer carry the verdict. Edits / cuts are shown separately as a manipulation indicator, not a deepfake layer: a heavily edited video is assembled content, not one continuous take. The Morse layers are listed too, with their decoded messages — the blink Morse, the audio Morse from the soundtrack's tones, and a row comparing the two — they are pattern decodes, not population averages, so they never add to the anomaly total; a deliberately coded pattern, or a match / disagreement between the two channels, is flagged in its own row instead.

Short clips score too — but not on breathing, prolonged closures, or gaze breaks. The minimums scale to the clip length instead of demanding a long recording for the layers that carry the verdict: blink rate and time between blinks score from about 2 seconds with a face tracked, blink duration scores from the first blink counted, and edits / cuts are shown as counts only and never score. Breathing, prolonged closures, and gaze breaks do not count toward the flag — a rate built from two or three breaths, or a single closure over 500 ms, is too little evidence to call a clip on, so all three are reported as counts only. Gaze breaks are counts only at any clip length — calibration on 24 genuine and 36 fake clips showed the two overlap fully, so look-away counts are reported, not scored (see its row). Rates on a short clip are extrapolated from the seconds actually measured, so they carry wider uncertainty than the same rate over a minute — and one guard stays in place: a low blink rate is only called outside the average once at least 8 seconds of face time has passed, because a normal gap between blinks already runs 2–10 seconds, so a few blink-free seconds prove nothing on their own.

Patterns are the full International (ITU) Morse set — letters, digits, and punctuation (see the Morse key in the results column)
Voice cadence, pitch, tremor & spectrumNo population average is applied to these as verdicts — they are reported as measurements with this page's stated flag tests. Speech cadence: natural bursts and pauses vary unevenly; a run of 6+ bursts with both varying under 13% is flagged as synthetic cadence. Pitch: natural pitch glides; a step over ±38% within 0.25 s during continuous speech is flagged. Micro-tremor: healthy pitch jitter is roughly a few tenths of a percent to about 1% (clinical voice literature); a 1.5 s voiced window under 0.18% jitter with a flat loudness envelope is flagged as tremor absent. Spectrum: within a sentence the recording chain is constant; a spectral-centroid jump over 45% held 300 ms+ mid-sentence is flagged
Voice Lie Detector — stress signals (combined)A voice lie detector, but not fully 100% accurate. These stress cues — micro-tremors, pitch/frequency shifts, intensity changes and spikes, speech-rate changes, and pausing — are commonly associated with lying and are the same cues used in voice lie detectors, but they do not prove someone is lying and stress has honest causes too; no population average and no lying threshold exists, so this is a pattern score, not a proof. Per speech segment: micro-tremor = pitch jitter (%); pitch/frequency shifts = F0 step changes over 25% plus F0 variability; intensity = volume (RMS) variation, swings, and spikes (a sudden jump to 2.2×+ the previous moment); speech rate = estimated syllables per second (calm speech is roughly 4 syllables/s), rate shifts within the segment or away from the clip's typical rate, plus pausing — long pauses (0.9 s+), sudden pauses mid-sentence (0.45 s+ cutting in while speech continues), and pause clustering (3+ pauses within about 5 s). Each cue group contributes 0–25 to a combined 0–100 score; 55+ is an elevated stress pattern, and a segment where an intensity spike, a speech-rate shift, and unusual pausing fire together gets a combined flag with its timestamp
Morse code in audioNo population average applies — this is a decode of tones in the soundtrack, not a biometric. Audio timing adapts to the beeps' speed using standard Morse ratios: the shortest tone sets the unit; under 2 units is a dot, 2 units or more a dash; silences under 2 units join a character, 2–5 units end a character, over 5 units end a word (fixed 300 ms dot/dash split if all tones are one length). Same full International (ITU) set and the same run-together prosigns ([R], [AR], [CQ], [SOS]). The audio message is compared with the blink message: matching text in both channels, or two different decoded messages, is flagged in the comparison box and the verdict

Ranges are general population averages of the kind published in clinical and university references (e.g., blink-rate literature summarized by Mayo Clinic and Harvard Health). Individuals vary widely — fatigue, medication, contact lenses, camera angle, and frame rate all move these numbers.

⚠ Read this before you use the numbers

This is a highly accurate detector. It gives statistics and normal biometrics for the average person — not a guarantee, and not proof. An unusual eye pattern does not prove a video is fake, manipulated, or AI-generated, and a normal-looking pattern does not prove a video is real. Lighting, camera angle, low frame rate, compression, glasses, and the person simply being tired can all produce “unusual” results, and the best manipulated videos may show none.

The same caution covers the new checks. A look-away is ordinary human behavior, and an edit or a stutter does not prove manipulation — most published video is legitimately edited, and freezes can come from the original recording, compression, or a struggling device. Micro-expressions are not lie detection — brief facial movements do not prove deception or fakery, and reading emotion from them is scientifically contested. A lip-sync mismatch has everyday causes too — voiceover, dubbing, an off-camera speaker, or a platform re-encode shifting the audio a few frames. Only the overall pattern, weighed with everything else, means anything. The Morse decode carries the same caution in the other direction: a blink sequence that can be read as International Morse does not by itself prove anyone intended to send a message — short natural sequences can decode by chance, which is why the coded flag requires a clustered, mostly-valid, multi-letter pattern rather than a single decodable group. The same applies to the audio decode: a notification chime, an equipment beep, or a held music note can register as a tone element, and a short tone sequence can decode by chance — and a match or disagreement between the blink and audio messages is only as strong as the two decodes behind it, so weigh the message lengths before reading anything into either flag. The voice-pattern flags carry the same caution: steady script-reading can look like synthetic cadence, an edit between two genuine takes steps the pitch and the spectrum exactly like a splice of fake audio, and heavy noise reduction or compression can flatten a real voice's micro-tremor — each flag is a moment to inspect, not a conclusion.

Stress is not the same as lying. The Voice Lie Detector layer shows stress and arousal patterns only — micro-tremors, pitch/frequency shifts, intensity changes and spikes, speech-rate changes, and pausing moving together, including the combined real-time flag when an intensity spike, a speech-rate shift, and unusual pausing fire in the same segment. Those cues are commonly associated with lying and are the same cues used in voice lie detectors — but this detector is not fully 100% accurate, a stressed voice does not prove someone is lying, and a calm voice does not prove they are telling the truth. No voice cue, and no combination of voice cues into a score, proves deception. Stress has honest causes too: nervousness, fatigue, illness, emotion, and being recorded at all can produce exactly these stress patterns.

Use your own best judgment. Treat this as one signal to weigh alongside the source of the video, its context, and other checks — never as a verdict on its own.
