Count the blinks. Keep the video.

Bring a video you already have. This checker watches the eyes frame by frame — right here on your device — and counts blinks, winks, and unusual eye patterns. Its lead layer watches for micro-expressions — tiny involuntary twitches around the eyes and mouth — and checks them against the spoken cadence, alongside breathing, head, and posture layers. It also tracks gaze, logging every time the eyes break off the camera, and compares frame to frame to catch every edit and stutter, and checks whether the lips and the voice actually line up. Nothing is uploaded, nothing is saved, and the file is dropped from memory the moment you remove it.

How it works

  1. You bring the file. You pick a video already on your device. This page never pulls video from TikTok, Instagram, or anywhere else, and there is no browser plugin or screen overlay.
  2. Your device does the math. A face-mesh model runs locally in this tab, tracking 468 facial landmarks. From the eye landmarks it computes how open each eye is, frame by frame.
  3. Micro-expressions lead (idea: Alexa Plus). The eye/brow and mouth regions are watched against their own baselines for twitches that fire and fade in a fraction of a second, and each twitch is checked against the spoken cadence from the audio. Supporting layers track head tilt and turns, posture shifts, shoulder movement in the lower frame, and the rise-and-fall rhythm of breathing.
  4. Patterns become counts. Dips in eye-openness are classified as blinks, winks, or prolonged closures, plotted on the timeline, and compared with average human ranges.
  5. Gaze is tracked too. The iris position inside each eye shows where the person is looking. Every break off the camera lasting a quarter-second or more is timed, given a direction, and added to the same timeline and log.
  6. Frames are checked for edits. Consecutive frames are compared directly: a sudden near-total change signals a cut, and frames repeating unchanged signal a stutter or freeze. Both land on the timeline with everything else.
  7. Lips are checked against the sound (idea: Alexa Plus). Mouth openness is measured from the lip landmarks while the audio level is measured from playback through the browser's own audio tools — never recorded. Sound with a still mouth, or a moving mouth in silence, is flagged as a lip-sync mismatch with its timestamp.
  8. Then it's gone. Results live only in this page's memory. Remove the file — or close the tab — and the video and its data are dropped. Nothing is stored or sent anywhere.

Average ranges used for comparison

MeasureTypical average range
Blink rate at restAbout 15–20 blinks per minute for an average adult; lower when reading or watching a screen (often 5–7/min), higher in conversation
Blink durationAbout 100–400 ms per blink
Time between blinksRoughly 2–10 seconds at rest; a fixed stare well beyond that is uncommon in natural, unedited footage
WinksOccasional and usually deliberate; frequent one-eye closures can also come from head angle, lighting, or one eye leaving the frame
Gaze breaksLooking away from the camera is normal — people glance at notes, screens, and other people. Brief glances are constant in natural footage; a long unbroken stare, or breaking away every few seconds, is what stands out
Edits & stuttersUnedited single-take footage has no hard cuts and plays smoothly. Cuts, freezes, and repeated frames point to editing, a splice, or a playback/recording problem — not, by themselves, to fakery
Lip syncIn genuine on-camera speech, mouth movement and sound rise and fall together, usually within about 40–100 ms of each other. Repeated stretches where one moves without the other — or a consistent offset well beyond that — are what this layer flags
Micro-expressionsBrief involuntary facial movements lasting roughly 40–500 ms occur in natural speech and reaction. There is no "normal count" — what this layer reports is where they land relative to the spoken cadence
Breathing rate at restAbout 12–20 breaths per minute for an average adult at rest, in a fairly regular rhythm; talking, exertion, and emotion all move it

Ranges are general population averages of the kind published in clinical and university references (e.g., blink-rate literature summarized by Mayo Clinic and Harvard Health). Individuals vary widely — fatigue, medication, contact lenses, camera angle, and frame rate all move these numbers.

⚠ Read this before you use the numbers

This tool gives statistics and normal biometrics for the average person — not a guarantee, and not proof. An unusual eye pattern does not prove a video is fake, manipulated, or AI-generated, and a normal-looking pattern does not prove a video is real. Lighting, camera angle, low frame rate, compression, glasses, and the person simply being tired can all produce “unusual” results, and the best manipulated videos may show none.

The same caution covers the new checks. A look-away is ordinary human behavior, and an edit or a stutter does not prove manipulation — most published video is legitimately edited, and freezes can come from the original recording, compression, or a struggling device. Micro-expressions are not lie detection — brief facial movements do not prove deception or fakery, and reading emotion from them is scientifically contested. A lip-sync mismatch has everyday causes too — voiceover, dubbing, an off-camera speaker, or a platform re-encode shifting the audio a few frames. Only the overall pattern, weighed with everything else, means anything.

Use your own best judgment. Treat this as one signal to weigh alongside the source of the video, its context, and other checks — never as a verdict on its own. This is a 24-hour proof of concept, not a forensic tool.
